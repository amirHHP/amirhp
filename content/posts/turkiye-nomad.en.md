(Part one): People in Turkey insist, to a degree that is hard to believe, on not understanding English. They will not even take *one* and *two* until you show it with your hands. I say they *will not* understand, because with a Latin script and this volume of tourists, you would speak English better than an Iranian even if you made no effort at all.

In Turkey you meet two groups. One turns their face away the moment they realize you are speaking English (the Grand Bazaar, for example). The other tries to get your work done (mostly younger people).

Across the city you see more Turkish than English, and only a few menus have English as well. Still, don’t worry: learn four or five letters and you can read the Turkish script the way you read English. Understanding speech is another matter (unless you already speak Turkish).

Most Turkish universities teach in Turkish. Some programs have an English track (maybe 30–40 percent), but English is always far more expensive. Jobs, except at large international companies, list in Turkish and interview in Turkish.

Note: under Atatürk’s push in 1932, Turkey’s script moved from Arabic letters to Latin ones. Imagine trying that with Persian — how many people would tear their clothes. Atatürk, with various groups behind him, announced the change at Dolmabahçe Palace and took the first step toward joining the world.

(Part two): The sea is a sea of gifts. It feeds you, it moves you, it is a pleasure to watch, it gives power, and it entertains. I think people who have no sea have lost a great deal in life.

People in Istanbul still spend their evenings by the water, and it matters that you can reach the sea from everywhere, even from the center of the city. Few cities have this kind of access.

Now that the weather is warmer, people sit by the sea for hours, drink, and talk.

Some, with a stick and a line, get all the protein they need from that same sea for free, or they make money from it.

The sea keeps tourists busy; dozens of tours run every day. Ships move thousands of passengers every hour, and people light up at the sight of the water.

That attachment is not without reason. Wars were fought on this sea, and crowds of people died in it. The Ottomans were all conquerors and vanquished of the sea.
