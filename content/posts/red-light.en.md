More than forty people died because two passenger trains collided, and more were injured.

The event is bitter and sharp enough. I do not intend to cleanse the people at fault, or to make the accident look trivial. My point is the same as the headline. Probably we too woke up one day, went to work, got busy, got bored, swore under our breath, and did a job half-heartedly. In the end we either escaped the result of the mistake, or we got out with a few apologies and an “I was wrong.” If we were very unlucky, in the worst case we were fired.

That day, for the line official, was like the everyday of many of us. He must have thought: for years his eyes have been glued to the trains’ path, and his whole life has gone to watching and worrying about an accident that never happened. He must have thought: how long am I supposed to keep doing this tired, repeating work? When one of the lights went red he must have thought: in all these years how many times has a train crashed on this line? He must have run the rail-accident stats in his head, and like many of us who think in the day “there must be a mistake” or “the software got it wrong,” the same idea came to him. Check the light? In that cold, who feels like going outside to check a light? You see: this is all us.

The air gets polluted? Like always we go to work in a private car and think “who feels like going out in this cold without a car,” or “one car won’t change anything.” Or the cement-factory boss, reading the death stats from air pollution, thinks “if I shut the factory for a day, then what? Who takes the loss?” He too was one of the people who killed, but nobody says he should have done his job properly, because we have gotten used to looking at every event in one way.

Today when I talked about the train collision in a group and tried to take this same approach (that is, to say you can look at the story another way), everyone puffed their chests and named the line official the first accused. I am not saying he was not at fault, but any of us could have been in his place and caused the deaths of dozens of people. Some people in today’s argument said “he should have paid attention and done his job right.” Sometimes we even pull ourselves further from the event: “his work is much more important, he is responsible for people’s lives.”

I want to say that however bitter this is, it is not strange or unpredictable. We have all said to ourselves one day, “God willing, this time nothing will happen.”

We all once thought the light had stayed red because of the cold.
