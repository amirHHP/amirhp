When I actually talked with them, their front sometimes shifted. About 30 percent of the people who started with insults and mockery entered a real conversation after I answered and invited them in. In that encounter I kept thinking: what did they do to us that we lost the tools of discussion? When we take sides like this over a computer system that sits miles from our identity — as if our identity had been attacked (and maybe some people have made Apple into their identity) — how are we going to talk on the day of freedom, when every kind of thinking is in the ring?

A few problems with my thread:

- I had not explained the context well. I wrote about my first encounter with a Mac, from notes I had made at the time (say six months earlier). A lot of what I wrote had been solved, or did not matter, but in that first encounter it was surprising or negative for me. So many people started offering workarounds or saying I didn’t know how, when the issue was not knowing how. The issue was a user’s *first encounter* with a product.

{{< tweet user="ArumBash" id="1635640779992453120" >}}

- Because I was using old notes, I really slipped in places. Epic Games, for example, or other software: I don’t know why I expected to find it in the store (you usually cannot download a store from another store). Maybe I expected the Apple ecosystem to be complete, and that if other ecosystems had gaps, this one would not. Maybe those first feelings had also faded and changed with time.

{{< tweet user="checkm1000" id="1635660975771643905" >}}

- I had one important aim. Something that had trapped me, and I did not want others trapped in it: “Apple is not what the ads say, so don’t pine for it.” Think about buying or not buying it apart from the mood of the moment. For a long time I thought I was missing a lot, when at most it is 20 percent better than other products.

- Small things were mixed with large things. That made a reader hit something silly and dismiss the whole piece. The priority of the critiques was also unclear. It started with logging into a Mac, which was not necessarily the worst experience.

![image-14020125125549675](https://amirhpnew.s3.ir-thr-at1.arvanstorage.ir/image-14020125125549675.png)

- I did not leave room for a fair critique. I could have written about the good parts somewhere, to give a sense of fairness. I did not.

For someone who, as a journalist, had written close to thirty reviews of different products and used to observe those rules, this was new. In those reviews everything was chosen carefully; you tried not to upset the maker; the critique was very fine, and sometimes self-censored. I remember we tried a few times to leave that frame, and we could not.

Problems with the replies:

- **You don’t know how; you should have done it this way.** A good product does not need a manual. It shows itself the right way. I tried to approach it as a new user with old habits, who first tries to do things the old way. So “the shortcut was X” is not a valid approach. I also tried to compare only basic things, copy and so on, and not complain that the start menu is like this or the close button is somewhere else or minimize is over there, and to stay on what was actually strange. My assumption was that this encounter should not require a search. After a search I learned how cut works, but my encounter was: I hit the usual shortcuts, they failed, I right-clicked, the option was not there. That is a broken experience.

{{< tweet user="amir_hhp" id="1637095521746649088" >}}

- **It worked for me.** I don’t know, but that does not matter. A correct product works for everyone. If it works for some people, that does not mean it is good and everyone else should somehow fix their problem. Some of the suggestions I was given I also found in searches.

It may be worth saying the good experiences I had in this time:

- The MacBook Air is extremely light and covered most of my needs. I no longer had to carry a laptop and a tablet, or take only the tablet and worry about its limits. I took a few trips with only a MacBook and an iPhone, and even when I had no power for two days it did not become a problem.
- If you have enough RAM, performance is good and the experience is smooth. Mine has 8 GB, which Chrome fills quickly and the machine stutters. In the latest Chrome, with tabs deactivating, it felt somewhat better, though I lost a feature.
- The devices work together well enough. There are bugs and snags, but in places the experience is genuinely clean. AirDrop is clean. Copy and paste across devices usually works.
- A Mac is not as closed as iOS. You can do all kinds of things with it. It has a command line. You can touch system settings and install all kinds of software, large and small.
- The Apple Watch has a terrible battery, and the sync with the iPhone is odd (I expected podcasts I downloaded on the phone to be available downloaded on the watch; they were not). Still, notifications and using the watch are fine. It does not have the strange extra apps I expected compared with rivals; it is mostly limited to the core uses.
- The iPhone camera takes reasonably sharp photos, and more important, it has excellent stabilization, and the mic works well, so it can be a good tool for vlogs and other video.
