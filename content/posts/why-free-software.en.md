It has been about two months since I moved, after years, from openSUSE to Mint. For a long time I could not find a distro that would convince me to drop my openSUSE habits, until I found that missing piece. I wanted to write about Mint and the experience I have had with it, then I thought there might be someone who does not know what Linux is or what it is for. I remembered my own teenage years, how I tore through sites looking for anything about Linux, read it hungrily, and always wished someone would explain it more fully. So I thought if a teenage friend happens to read this, or finds it in a search, they deserve a complete piece. I will take this in three texts. This one is about free software; the next is about GNU/Linux.

## What is free software?

Every computer program is tens, thousands, or millions of lines of code, depending on its size. Some people decide to sell the code they wrote. Others prefer to make money another way. Those who want to sell the code naturally have to block access to it and close the source, so nobody looks at it and builds something similar. That is what Apple and Microsoft do with their operating systems: you never find out, when Windows breaks, which lines are wrong and what is happening under the shell. They will not let you know the inside of the system and the software you bought from them, or change it to fit your needs.

The other group believes you should not close users off from the code, because once you sell someone a product you should let them use it however they want and look into every corner. In their view, companies like Apple, Microsoft, Adobe, Oracle, Symantec, and the rest, by closing their code, stand like a dam against the progress of software and will not let others build better software on top of theirs. So they created the idea of free software and gave the user four rights:

> Users must be free to run the program for any purpose.
>
> Users must be free to change the program to fit their needs. To do that, the [source code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_code) must be available.
>
> Users must be free to redistribute the program. That can be free of charge or for money.
>
> If a user changes the program, they must be free to redistribute it again under the same free license. (For [copyleft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyleft) software, the source of the changed program must also be given to other users.)

## The main reason to use free software

The most important reason to use GNU/Linux and free software in general is that the culture and philosophy of software freedom respect you. You are not its slave. You can edit a free program and give it to anyone you want. You are not a criminal in the law’s eyes, and the maker sees you as a user, not a thief. When you use free software you do not have to do the ugly work of cracking, trying to break a lock. You do not have to say which country you are from. You have the best, highest-quality version, and you use it easily. That respect is the main reason I use free software. Alongside that, I believe the philosophy is a movement toward a software utopia: a place where you control the software, not a dystopia where the software controls you. Even if working with these programs is harder and slower (and in many cases it is the opposite), these philosophies and these firm aims make me choose free software when one side is free and the other is proprietary.

## Is open source frightening?

Last month the secretary of the Supreme Council of Cyberspace said in a comment that one reason it was easy to invade privacy on Telegram was that it is open source. We had heard such comments about open source before. The common argument is that because the code of free and open-source software is available to everyone, anyone can look at it and the ways in are open.

That danger is as logical as saying that because a murderer might be outside, you should never leave the house. Staying inside might keep you from possible murderers, but it makes you more vulnerable to many other dangers. You might die of a simple cold or an infection. Safety rules that have been followed for years, which you do not know, might threaten you. All of that is because we defined the problem wrong. Fear of being killed by a murderer because you left the house is wrong for a simple reason: in every society there have always been more good people than bad.

The same is true of the eyes watching free-software code. Before an attacker abuses a hole in open source, it is very likely that people who love that software and use it every day have reported the hole, and thousands of programmers around the world have fixed it.

What happens in closed-source software is worse. Holes are found that have existed for years, and nobody knows how many people abused them, and because few people had access to the source, the problem stayed hidden from everyone except attackers until very late.

Turning away from free software is like going into a cave of solitude. More than giving our IT industry a feeling of independence, it makes it vulnerable to the smallest attacks. The result is that a simple team of hackers can break into content-management systems written for Iranian sites and make the news. If those government sites, instead of reinventing the wheel, had trusted open systems like WordPress or Drupal and built on that, they would have had, at far lower cost, a system hardened by years of hard attacks, with code in front of millions of programmers who valued that free project more than their dinner. Civilized people have always advanced by standing on the shoulders of those who came before.
