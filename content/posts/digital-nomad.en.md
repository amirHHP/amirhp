I’ve spent a few days reading and thinking about digital nomads, and in this thread I want to say that for an Iranian — Iranian passport, rial income, no bank account — it “probably” does not look like those photos. Keep in mind this is less “travel” than “life,” and it has to feel like life.

In this way of living you live on the road. The upside is you leave the loop of “I’ll make money so that one day I can see the world.” So it matters that your costs look like ordinary life in Tehran. I chose couchsurfing: stay with locals and drop the housing cost.

At first I wanted to start in the Maldives. Why? Iranians can stay three months without a visa, one of the longest visa-free options. Then I saw how small the islands are, how new the country is, how little distinctive culture there is — and the flights cost around 20 million tomans.

Then I learned about digital-nomad visas. Most countries give them to people with high income. Five or six might still be in reach even after converting rials to dollars: Bermuda ($0), Brazil ($1500), Cape Verde ($1500), Colombia ($900), Curaçao ($0), Ecuador ($1350).

The high application fees and a two-year ceiling made me drop them for now. When you work remote, the real challenge is a stable connection and a stable mind. Couchsurfing or “let’s see what happens” will wreck that. So I looked for other paths.

By chance on Airbnb I saw places with strange discounts on monthly stays. Sri Lanka, which I had dropped because of visa cost, suddenly came back on the table. For $55 you can get a six-month visa, which is a good number.

The path (which might take a year) starts in Turkey, because tickets from Iran are cheap right now and it is a lower-risk first step. Through all of this, money is the main problem for an Iranian. You have no card, your income lands in rials in Iran, and you cannot even receive an SMS.

And I still have no real workaround. Some things, like flights and stays, you can apparently settle with an Iranian card and rial payment. Beyond that it is a serious mess, especially for a long stay. We’ll see whether exchanges will take crypto as a favor.

You also cannot take much money with you, and the money you do take has already been converted from something nearly worthless. So whatever clothes you pack, you live in them, and you cannot do expensive things. I say this so it is clear: for an Iranian this life is far from a nice tour. It is not impossible.

Airbnb recently shipped about fifty new features around “rooms,” positioning toward cheaper stays and more local advantages. They had started this earlier with Airbnb Experiences, which apparently did not take. Here I want to talk about my recent experience with the platform.

I mentioned digital-nomad life before: no fixed place, working and living on the road, backpack always on. I started, and a month later it is not what it was supposed to be — though it was not that hard either.

This was the house that started the trip: a clean villa with a pool in Sri Lanka for a mere $36 a month. We were one step from booking it when Nowruz hit, flights to neighboring countries got tempting, and instead we booked an old hotel in Istanbul.

And this was the hotel we booked in Istanbul. An old hotel that had nothing to do with its photos. No pool, no cleanliness. We arrived at midnight. A kid checked our details, checked us in, and the next morning they asked how we had even booked at that price.

I said that in the past year I had booked more than fifteen stays in different Iranian cities online without a single problem. I assumed that, like Iran, once you pay, it is done. Apparently that is not how Airbnb works. Support apologized and said it happens.

I never thought working with an Iranian product would feel better than an international one. For two years I took five- or six-day trips with [@otaghak](https://twitter.com/otaghak) every other month, and not once after booking and paying did a host go back on their word. On Airbnb, [@Airbnb](https://twitter.com/Airbnb), it happened twice in one day.

I don’t know why my default was so certain that something said inside a system this large and this polished must be true. I booked on the listed price, paid, and showed up. Wishful thinking. [@AirbnbHelp](https://twitter.com/AirbnbHelp)

We spent a week looking for a replacement. Maybe this was also my last attempt to prove that on a rial income you can live like an American digital nomad. It hit a wall. We messaged everyone this time.

We learned that every $20–$60 listing on Airbnb (about twenty in Istanbul) was wrong. A bug or a UX problem had applied a 90 percent monthly discount when the usual figure is 50 percent at most. Our costs jumped overnight.

We had wanted to start life on the road cheaply. The lowest Airbnb price for a month was $200–$300. We found a $190 place; that night they backed out and said take this other room for $320. We finally settled on a $240 room.

This is exactly where the Rooms feature lives: so-called private rooms inside a larger house. The host rents one or two rooms to cut costs. Kitchen, dishes, bathroom, hallway, and front door are shared. Each room has three or four pieces of furniture.

Sometimes the host lives there too. Sometimes they took the house as a business and rented every room, like our first month: four rooms, one person in each. A bed, a closet, a desk, a sofa, sometimes a TV.

I was very worried about internet, then I remembered that if I could live in an internet black hole I can probably live anywhere. Most rooms have unlimited internet. ADSL is about 14–20 Mbps; fiber is 100–120. Cafes and restaurants have internet too.

What we lived was far from “being a guest.” The host mostly left you alone. Airbnb seems to want more interaction, more knowing each other, more shared activity, something other than a dry empty room.

For anyone who wants this life: if you have a remote job of at least 15 million tomans, leave Iran. There is a decent chance you can spend a few months traveling, living, and working in a few cheap countries without worrying about a place to sleep.

With that budget we got a room, ordinary food, and about two days off a week to walk the city and eat out. In some countries (Turkey included) there are exchanges where you can give USDT and take lira, which makes moving money much easier.

Apart from that, Airbnb seems to want a real focus on long stays. Given interviews where the founder said every employee can work from anywhere in the world, it looks like they are trying to normalize a wide range of digital-nomad uses.
