Use of search engines grows every day. As the volume of information on the internet rises sharply every month, access to what is there also gets harder. In a near future nobody will be able to find their own content easily. Out of necessity, lost and thirsty, they will reach one of the search engines so it can take them to the boundless sea of information. So in a future that has more or less already started, search engines become the main gate people use to enter the internet and cyberspace. In that situation it is enough to put your hand on that gate’s throat to control a large part of the people. That is the value of search engines.

Contrary to what many think, the danger of search engines is not spying. It is their high ability to distort facts, and they do it much more effectively than other media. What more logical reason than telling someone “find such-and-such for me” and hearing in reply: “I looked, no such thing existed,” or, more dangerous, “I looked, but the results that fit your taste are only these.”

When you search a word on Google, Google, based on your habits and past behavior collected over years, shows you different results. That is, Google does not tell you the truth; it shows you what you like to see. At the end of that process you slowly sit in an information bubble and you never notice what is happening outside it. Exactly like what happens in countries whose media are under government control. The same thing happens to internet content. Every day you search more on Google, and every day you get more lost in the bubble it decides for you. Every day Google knows you better and makes the bubble smaller. You can never leave it, and it is better to say you never notice it exists, because things are as pleasant as you want them to be.

Google uses this for income. But countries like Iran that want to control media can, by implementing the same method, reach their aims: controlling information and blocking access to forbidden information.

When I was a teenager, the forbidden section of the library in Harry Potter had a special excitement for us. Reaching mysterious information that nobody had a right to know was interesting and, at the same time, very strange. A few years later, at the end of adolescence, I learned that this “forbidden section” also exists in reality. There were things nobody talked about, no book about them, and they were not easy to find even on the internet. Dark but important information, sitting in the back rooms of people’s minds and computer memory.
