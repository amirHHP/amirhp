Venezuela’s food inflation — **584.8 percent** in June 2026 versus June 2025 — is the pile-up of a year of currency pressure, heavy import dependence, and how the index is built. Talk of a “relative improvement” means a comparison with the hundreds-of-thousands-percent hyperinflations of earlier years, not a stable, cheap economy.

Headline inflation that same month, according to the Central Bank of Venezuela, was **544.1 percent** year on year. Food ran hotter than the average. It was not the only expensive part of the basket.

The main reasons food sits this high:

**Year-over-year inflation**

The June 2026 index is measured against June 2025, a year when the bolívar fell hard. Even when the monthly pace eases for a while, a year of accumulated jumps still sits inside the annual number. That number does not only climb: headline year-on-year inflation peaked at **649.5 percent** in March 2026, dropped to **524.5 percent** in May, then rose again.

**Food imports and the exchange-rate shock**

Imports make up about **60 percent** of the country’s food supply. A weaker bolívar feeds through quickly into rice, oil, flour, and meat. In a retail market anchored to the parallel dollar, pass-through is close to complete — not a measured coefficient of exactly 100 percent. Large deals are done in dollars; everyday food is repriced off the free rate in real time.

**Less cheap rationing, not a sudden price liberalization**

Formal price controls have been largely dead for years. What changed in 2026 is the winding-down of the cheap CLAP food boxes and a shift toward cash transfers. Households that relied on the in-kind ration lost that buffer. That is pressure on the food basket, not an overnight jump from a state price to a free-market price.

**Weak farms and expensive logistics**

Agriculture still cannot fill the domestic market after years of missing fuel, fertilizer, machinery, and power cuts. Inter-state freight pushes the final price higher. The central bank tied part of the July spike to distribution damage after the June 24 earthquakes.

“Stabilization,” so far, mostly means the system did not collapse and oil revenue returned at the macro layer. Getting that into farms, supply chains, and the food basket takes time, and the short run still comes with waves of higher prices.

To read the chart, keep **month-on-month (MoM)** inflation apart from **year-on-year (YoY)** inflation. The figures below are headline CPI, not food alone. The latest official print is July 2026.

1. **Monthly inflation** is the instant speed of price increases. It hit **32.6 percent** in January, in the shock after Maduro’s removal, then fell to **6.3 percent** in May — the lowest in 19 months. The Chevron and Shell contracts were signed in **April**, not May. June bounced to **13.8 percent**; July was **19.9 percent**.
2. **Year-on-year inflation** is still above 500 percent because the comparison is with 2025. It peaked in March, fell in May, then climbed again. Food was 584.8 percent in June and **609.7 percent** in July. Education was even higher in July (**647 percent**). Food, at about 32 percent of the CPI basket, is the heaviest everyday pressure on households — not always the single highest line in the table.

### After cooperation with the United States

* **Oil dollars and a relative FX calm (February to May):** With official exports reopening and the April Chevron and Shell deals, the central bank could intervene more in the currency market and pull monthly inflation toward single digits for a few months. The larger U.S. energy deal was announced in late August — after this data.
* **Food does not ease in isolation:** Transport, education, and services also jumped again in the summer. What households feel is still mostly food, because its weight in the basket is large and it is tied to imports and the free dollar.
* **An outlook, not a year-end miracle:** The IMF’s April 2026 WEO put average inflation near **387 percent** in 2026 and near **94 percent** in 2027. That is a gradual exit from hyperinflation, not headline inflation falling below 100–150 percent by late 2026.
