A few days ago a photo spread on social media of mandatory prayer at Hell Glue Factory, a name you had probably, like me, never heard. After that, thanks to transparency or the CEO’s showing off (or whatever we call it), a lot of information about the workplace and the rules was reshared from the factory’s Twitter account (how many factories do you know that have a Twitter account?), telling of the manager’s strange and sometimes anti-human rules and his “slavery.” I was one of the first people to mock this CEO (what an honor!) and I certainly condemn mandatory prayer, but I tried to look at the story from another angle as well.

From here the note splits in two. First, imagine the workers entered “by force” and against their will. That is, say that in that city there was absolutely no other reasonable job, and people had to work there for food, clothes, and housing, and the owner, knowing they had no other choice, opened his managerial complexes with strange rules and abused people. With that premise the rest of the argument is void, and I condemn the whole affair. There is no doubt his work was unjust, and though becoming the only entrepreneur in town and creating jobs for a few hundred people on his own is valuable (if we assume he used no rent), abusing his position is unacceptable. I have not lived working conditions in a small city, nor had every path in front of me closed, so I am not the right person to understand part of that situation.

Still, let us look at the second path. Set aside forced labor and look more at the judgments made about this story. Assume people had at least two other job choices and entered the factory knowing the rules. Assume the conditions or the pay or other benefits were good enough that they accepted the rules. There is a lot to say here. **The rest of this piece is not a cleansing of that CEO.** It tries more to look at mistakes people may make toward **other entrepreneurs or factory owners**.

## Why keep reading?

Seeing a story from the angle most people criticize is always interesting. We can always judge from the defaults in our heads, but a conversation forms when different sides of the argument are grown and stretched. That so many condemned the whole affair without enough knowledge, without knowing all the working conditions, shows the ugly face of social media and people’s emotional joining-in.

## Why did I enter this?

I wanted to open this because of the criticism and mockery aimed at young startups. A self-made startup at the start does not have much money to pay people; the main capital is the company’s future. Some people treated that as unpaid labor and mocked a startup that wanted to hire someone for less than 500,000 tomans, or even zero (and a share of future equity). My argument there was that the person has a choice: take no cash in a company where there is room to grow, or a friendly environment, or equity.

## Is entrepreneurship easy?

The discussion started with this tweet: “Really one of our biggest problems is that we always take the employees’ side. The person is paying 300 people; we don’t see that, we see the strange rules.”

A common argument was “it’s not free money, he’s paying them for their work.” Anyone who knows the twists of entrepreneurship knows what a huge volume of work paying three hundred people means. If an entrepreneur earns enough to cover space, tax, workers, marketers, factory equipment, running costs, and possible losses, it means at the start they bowed to dozens of people, passed on profit many times, went months with no income, and saw their assets hit zero more than once. Anyone who has not had that cannot understand the nervous pressure. After that much pain they have the right to have their own factory, their own methods, their own rules, and to set those rules for more profit. (Again: assuming people had a choice to work with them.) All these years, management methods and factory rules around the world have been for higher productivity. Even now, when worker freedom at work is respected, it is for higher productivity, not human rights.

Some people called that sentence the evil nature of capitalism. The point is that capitalism, unlike other respectable and attractive systems, has worked for years. In the world we have, whether ideas work matters more than how beautiful and humane they are. “Capitalism” also comes from our human nature; denying it is denying human desires and ourselves.

## Are this factory’s rules unjust?

Clearly no. My argument is that when the workers have accepted these conditions (if they had freedom), they have no problem with the strictness and the many strange rules. A lot of what looks unjust to us may look very logical to a worker there. We are certainly not in a position to interpret that injustice. Hard conditions mean something different for each person.

## Are the rules irrational?

Some of the rules are extremely strange. What you can clearly take from it is that the CEO has tried to learn factory-owning purely by experience, and has made many mistakes on the way. It is clear that every rule came from a real event: a problem happened, and the CEO tried to add a rule to prevent it in the future. Photos from the factory (the staging, fake plants to make the space different, workers at birthday parties) show that with his incomplete ability and mindset he tried to make a different space for the workers and pay attention to them. Right or wrong, it lands on his own profit and loss.

## Are the workers unhappy with their conditions?

Clearly nobody knows. No independent report of work at the factory has been published. That is the pain: nobody knows whether the workers are unhappy. Everyone tries to look at it from their own ideals. Suppose all the workers are people who pray; then what does it matter if there are rewards and punishments for it? They pray of their own will. In the same tweet many compared it to North Korea, a comparison too shallow to be worth examining.

## Does the manager violate the workers’ privacy?

Some people said this method violates privacy. Privacy does not even have a single definition. That you cannot touch your phone at work may be a privacy violation for you and completely accepted for a worker. Should we try to make the worker understand that they have a right to use their phone at work and nobody has a right to ask?

Or that you have twenty minutes on the clock for lunch, or that after the break nobody has a right to stop working. All of that is fully accepted for a worker, and not for you who work at a private company in Tehran.

## Should the government and the law step in?

To answer that you have to understand one thing well: an entrepreneur is also a human being who, if they have not suffered more than the worker to get where they are, has not suffered less (an entrepreneur without rent, of course). Suppose something happens here, and for example the CEO is forced to change the rules, or under government pressure the factory’s structure turns into the traditional, common Iranian model. In the end that hits the entrepreneur’s motivation, control leaves their hands, and the factory fails. Five hundred people lose their jobs, and the town’s economy goes quiet.

Government entry is certainly a disruption of the worker–employer balance. If the government makes the rules stricter, an entrepreneur will not want to enter the game and will prefer to use their capital another way. The only solution to these problems is not a top-down demand that wrecks the game and the structure, but workers knowing their rights and stating demands from the bottom up. That way the employer–worker system evolves properly, and the employer is forced to offer better conditions and more freedom at work to keep workers.
