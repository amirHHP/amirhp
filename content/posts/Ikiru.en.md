> “Now and then I think of my own death, and in that state I fall to thinking how I can bear the last breath at all, while I have this kind of life, how I can leave it. I feel there are many other things I must do. I have the feeling I have lived very little, and at that time I sink into thought. But I do not become sad. It was from such a feeling that *Ikiru* came into being…”

*Ikiru* is one of those films that stay for a lifetime. Because those old questions came back to the front of my mind. What is the value of being alive? When should we pull the brake on routine and stop? Are we forever slaves of the system we were formed in? How long does slavery at work go on?

![](http://stephenesherman.com/images/ikiru-patients.jpg)

“To Live” (whose story Kurosawa took from Tolstoy’s *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*) tries to answer these questions with the story of Watanabe, a middle-aged clerk who has spent thirty years in a tiring office job. One day he suddenly looks death in the eye. Stomach cancer will finish him in six months.

Stop here. Put yourself in this person’s place. Take your eyes off the monitor for a moment and think: if you learned you had only a few months left, what would “living” mean? I think this is a level of awareness. Your whole world changes. When you look at a “tree,” do you see the same “tree,” or does facing death turn your worldview over?

Here is the viewer’s first meeting with “the individual” as a deciding element in society, with Watanabe, whose terminal illness has of course already been established, and the first meeting with “society,” the women who have come to complain.

(Women’s voices: “My child got sick from the water… the water is foul… there are millions of mosquitoes…”) These phrases also point to the illness of society. In this state the narrator, after this visual parameter, continues introducing Watanabe, who looks at his watch (as a device showing the passing of time, whose value Watanabe does not know): “He is wasting time… it is hard to say that he is living…”

Even after facing death, when the clerk of the story goes to his child, the child’s coldness makes him lose hope. Even drinking and pleasure until morning does not make him well. That is how Watanabe decides to spend the rest of his life on something that matters; not for himself, but for others. The result is a return to unfinished work he had ignored in his clerk years. A simple, maybe small job: persuading a complex administrative bureaucracy to turn a muddy pond into a children’s park.

## The periods of Ikiru, or one human’s worldview

In the film we see three periods of Watanabe’s life. In the first he has spent a long time working, saved all his income, and taken no pleasure from life. A dry, lonely man who after his wife’s death devoted himself to his son and went near no other woman.

![](https://images-na.ssl-images-amazon.com/images/I/81yVROr1dXL._SL1500_.jpg)

After knowing about death, Watanabe enters the second period of his life. One night in a cabaret the old man starts singing a poem, a poem called “Life is Brief.” In this sequence we still see on his face the tears, the grief, and the unsolved dissatisfaction with his life. The third period is about the large decision he makes. He wants to follow the building of the park, but there is no life left, and we hang in whether he succeeded or not. The film tells of the human being and his constant challenge: having a purpose, or his final end.

After Watanabe’s death we reach the municipal clerks. Through the film, while Watanabe is alive, they only blame him and talk behind his back, and after they are sure of his achievement they fall into worship of the dead and, drunk, swear they will continue his path, exactly like the ancestor-worshipping people we see every day. The last sequences at the mourning ceremony are truly excellent. Consecutive flashbacks, memories told by different people, and humans who until a moment ago were all mocking Watanabe suddenly praise him.

Akira Kurosawa, director of *Ikiru*, is a master of genius and creativity in decoupling. In one shot, when the man decides to transform his life, a doll in his hand that a young girl has given him, he comes down the stairs. In the background a birthday is being held; guests come and start congratulating the birthday in such a way that the viewer thinks they are speaking to Watanabe, the old man. He moves away from the camera and someone whose birthday it is enters the frame. The action is justified that way, but we know they were speaking to Watanabe and congratulating his second birth. *Ikiru* is not a film about death. It is about living, like its name. It is not about a human’s fear of death; it is about his fear of not living.
