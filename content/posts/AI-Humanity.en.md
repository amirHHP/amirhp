“It is predicted that by 2050 emotional and sexual relationships between robots and humans will become ordinary.” True Companion has now promised to build “the world’s first sex robot” and said its first product will enter the market by the end of the year. The product already has thousands of pre-orders. (quoted from BBC Persian)

Apart from the ethics, this story reminds me more than anything of Ray Kurzweil’s predictions. A man who years ago forecast the path of technology with a fair approximation, and now we are standing, in a way, inside his old dreams. He talks of a time when the present human brain falls behind, a time when, with brain-transfer technology, immortality comes to humans, and even if the body wears out and dies, they can keep the main part of themselves — the brain — alive, and notice no death. He worked at Google for a while and now, at a university he founded, follows his old dream of “futures studies.”

I was talking with someone about the power of AI. The argument was that I believed artificial intelligence and robotics would eventually destroy humanity. He, like others, accused me of pessimism and said it would not happen, because the same humans who created AI and developed it would also stop its development. I could have agreed, but I thought he had surely forgotten Hiroshima, the arrival of atomic bombs, destructive computer viruses, biological wars, and many inventions made by humans that very quickly left their control.

I talked with someone else about AI reaching human intelligence. He believed that because AI is built on mathematics and is material, it can never reach human intelligence, which has extra-material veins. Whether the mind and brain are extra-material is itself hours of argument, but he had probably forgotten the progress of science, and forgotten that five years ago companions like Siri and Cortana were still at the level of science-fiction films, and nobody thought you would so soon talk to your phone in conversational language. This group of people think we will one day hit a technology wall. Looking at the past (when we hit the wall of vacuum tubes and replaced them with the transistor), I think there is no wall for technology. I look for the reason more here: technology, which mostly came to make life easier, comes from human imagination and dreams, and human dreams are the least stationary part of the universe.

Kurzweil said: “Today our software cannot grow, because it is trapped in a human brain of only 100 trillion connections and synapses. In the future the life of our software will no longer depend on the survival of our brain’s computational circuits. Imagine a world where the difference between human and machine fades.”
