“A beginning” is a series about how Mibarim took shape, and the path I took in those few years. Part one is [here](/en/posts/startup-story-1-school-scape/). This part is about an unexpected event that changed my work: Lean Startup Machine.

I am twenty. With a little journalism experience, I stumble into the startup event Lean Startup Machine. I am supposed to cover the opening as a journalist, but the night before I think: “What if I get a chance to pitch my idea?” Can I turn the dream of starting a company into something real there? Is it not time to make a current instead of writing down other people’s success?

That night I start thinking about an old idea I liked: a health-control and calorie-tracking system. My mother and father both moved very little, and they thought a short walk and housework was enough activity.

So I thought of building that system. I named it that same night: “Sepas: health monitoring system,” so it would look as if I had thought of everything. I knew nothing about building it. No Iranian team was working on it then, and for almost two years nobody else entered the space.

Thursday afternoon I arrived later than the others. Everyone was pitching. Upset that I had missed the game, I tried to throw myself in. I told [Nasser Ghanemzadeh](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ghanemzadeh/) (who was running it) and we agreed that if I came all three days and wrote a good report, I could join (in the end, besides tweets, no print report of the event was published). Once we agreed I went on stage fast to pitch, in a situation I had never been in.

Thirty ideas were presented. Mine, to my disbelief, was chosen as one of the top eight. When I came off the stage I was stunned. Even that much was heavy: I had come for something else, I pitched, I was chosen, and people came up to me on their own: “I think your idea was better than all the others. Can I be on your team?”

## Everything becomes real at Lean Startup Machine

In three minutes a five-person team formed. We started on Sepas. By chance, three of them had been sent by their company to see what was happening, and their goals were different from mine. I had thrown myself into the fire to get into the main process.

Those three days are a real turning point. I go to the park, I talk to people to see if they like the idea. I have to get close to strangers in a few minutes, which for me is impossible. Even less believable: I take 5,000 tomans from three people for an app that does not exist. That is where I understand what it means when a product has paying customers before it is built.

[![Lean Startup Machine board](http://amirhp.ir/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/IMAG04192.jpg)](http://amirhp.ir/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/IMAG04192.jpg)

In the end we won as the only team that had taken cash from users for the idea. I will never forget the moment a judge asked, “Can we see the money you took?” and I pulled those few thousand tomans out of my pocket and the room erupted. A strange, memorable afternoon.

## Trying to give birth

After that I went door to door trying to make Sepas real. I had no picture of how you start a startup. I asked everyone I knew to find an Android developer, I went to a high-school friend, I tried to learn it myself, I tried hybrid models. I failed every time. People would first say they wanted to work on it; when they realized there was no cash, a thousand excuses appeared. About five people, in five different stretches, were supposed to join. All of them ran.

Sometimes you learn things you did not expect. At a web and mobile festival, from one of the talks, I found the missing piece: a cofounder. I needed someone who would put their whole mind on the startup, not just write code.

This time I tried a different way of convincing people. I used friends and classmates, until I persuaded one person to join not as a programmer but as a cofounder with real responsibility and a real share, someone who would push me if I slackened. For about six months we tried to work that way. We went to one Avatech session and were rejected because we were out of sync and had no particular advantage.

We moved slowly and without aim. Next to class we had no serious motive. Most of our time went to school and other work, as if neither of us believed anything particular would happen. Eventually the cofounder started postponing things, then took a full-time job, and the project stopped.

After that I tried to use what I myself could do. I don’t know why, but the “rebrand” disease caught us too. I decided “Sepas” was not the right name, and after much thought I changed it to Lifez. With Behnaz Tohidi we tried to start Lifez’s social channels so we would have users before the product existed.

In the end my year of trying to birth Lifez went nowhere, because of a small network, an inability to convince people, an inability to show a vision, and weak management. Lifez died before it was born, though successful products like Karafs and Mannequin now do everything I had in mind.
