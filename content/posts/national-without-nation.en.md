The word “national” became valuable for us once we grew sensitive to the appearances of being national. We stuck it on everything so a little national zeal would boil, and so we could take money for grand conferences and projects. Projects that sometimes had no economic justification, but on the excuse of being “national” could justify both the media and the costs.

If we set aside the “national car” and the “national satellite” and the rest, it happened plenty in IT too: from a large project called “national internet” (and even after they changed the name and called it the more reasonable “National Information Network,” they still would not drop “national”) to other projects like a national operating system, whose fate nobody still knows.

Later, when the word went dull, a new word, “indigenous,” took its place. Now we hear “indigenous search engine,” “indigenous content,” and “indigenous social network.” A while ago a project called “Comprehensive Portal of Online Social Networks” was unveiled.

It was in truth an open-source social network that had even been translated into Persian before. This time they had not even bothered to localize it. The project was ordered by the Information Technology Organization and prepared by Sharif University, but a look at user feedback on opening day was enough to see that all the old problems — distrust and mockery — were still there. Not one person showed the project a kind face.

At that opening I asked the head of the IT Organization why such a project had been launched, and what need there was for it. I said that if a company had a logical, profitable business model that actually needed a social network, it would certainly consider Axolotl as one option itself. But a government putting a government budget at the base of such software produces something like the national OS or the other indigenous and national projects whose names we have heard many times.

At the same event there was someone who had worked on his own social network for years, and was now upset at the government’s one-sided cheerleading, and throughout the ceremony kept saying how this so-called “national” project had affected his contract with the municipality.

Now privacy is pulled in too, and people who fear the government’s entry into their private lives more than they fear the absence of privacy.

Maybe the solution to “indigenizing” is, more than government pressure, to **let go**. Let social networks and communication software come from the people for the people, not from the government for the people.

Successful paths in the past can always light the way. The largest Persian online encyclopedia, without carrying the suffix “national” and without government help, was written by Persian speakers and is now the seventh-largest encyclopedia in the world.
