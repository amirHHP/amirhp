In Iran, startups have become the chicken of every feast and funeral. Whatever happens, it somehow ties back to startups. From the other side, even people you expect to know better say strange things. People usually start opining about startups without knowing what a startup is. In this piece I say why a lot of the complaints do not land.

## 1. The idea of a startup in Iran is nothing but a show

It is natural that anything new, when it enters a society, has supporters and opponents. Startups were one of those ideas and cultures. When they arrived they pulled people in, and because the growth was explosive and fast, many took a stand against them. I was one of those who thought the whole thing was a bubble, and I said so in [a note in Esfand 1394](/en/posts/startup-bubble/). My mistake was looking from outside the ring, and looking radically. Startups do come with a lot of show, but what you get in the end is valuable enough that you can forget the shows before it. What happened to Iranian startups was only a transition that happens in every society and every ecosystem. As time passes, Iran’s business and entrepreneurship space is maturing, and the players are learning their duties and professional behavior. The hardness of the work also keeps people who cannot do it from going that way.

## 2. Nine out of ten startups fail

The sentence is somewhat true. It gets stretched, though, when people use it to mock starting a new business, and they miss a point: that failure rate probably exists in any new project. If you decide to start a news site, if you want to build a Telegram channel and make money from it, if you want to start any vague project without much capital, you have a high chance of failing. So the rate is not unique to startups. It belongs to any project started this way: the founder has little capital, wants to start from zero, and there will be a lot of trial and error. That nine of ten startups fail is not a sign that the culture or the method is weak. It is a sign of how valuable the one that remains is.

## 3. If starting a startup were profitable, everyone would do it

Entrepreneurship, leadership, and years spent on one project need a particular kind of nerve. Not everyone can last in the twists of starting a company. In a startup you spend months on one problem, without being sure the work is right or that decent money will come from it. That makes you very fragile. Many of the people you add to the team with great difficulty cannot bear that pressure and vagueness, and they leave you quickly. From the outside, starting a startup may look interesting and exciting, but even people who are sure of their abilities often quit this way of working after a year.

## 4. When a mature company exists, why struggle in a startup?

This too is relative, and the same view shows up in other places. In the Linux world people always say working on an existing distro is more rational than building a new one, or in traditional business they say working in a mall is better than an independent shop. Small businesses are the most important players in a living economy. They show you can start something and become strong without government money and central power. The more ready a society and an economy are for starting from zero, the more room that society has to bloom.

## 5. Why work somewhere that pays no salary for months? Life has costs

Starting a company has one important challenge: no income for months. You may have no income for two years, and besides savings you need to be mentally ready. That does not mean the situation stays that way forever. You can almost say with confidence that a well-paid future is waiting. Even if your first business fails, you eventually find the way, and in the third or fourth you succeed. Then, after a few years, you earn far more than in the hard years you endured. Of course nothing is certain.
