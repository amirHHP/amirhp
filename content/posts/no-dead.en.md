On an ordinary day, scrolling through the usual empty Facebook posts, I saw someone had written a note regretting the death of one of my friends. At first I thought they were kidding, but when I looked at the comments I saw it was serious. Some offered condolences; some did not even bother and settled it with a colon and an open parenthesis. The situation was real, and I learned through Facebook that a good friend of mine had died.

From that day for a week I saw all kinds of condolence messages placed on the deceased’s “wall” in my “timeline.” Every day I opened Viber, his photo was there next to the living people, and under it it said last seen a week ago. He was still there. He was still in Viber, still had a Facebook profile… he was still alive.

A few days later when I went back to Facebook, a box opened in front of me: “It’s so-and-so’s birthday, congratulate them.” He was dead, but for Facebook it makes no difference. A dead person is still a user.

[![dead_end1](http://amirhp.ir/wp-content/uploads/2014/11/dead_end1.jpg)](http://amirhp.ir/wp-content/uploads/2014/11/dead_end1.jpg)

In the past, social relations ended when a person reached the point of death. Their things and keepsakes were gathered and put aside, the house was sold, and after three or four anniversaries they were forgotten completely. Now social networks, by removing the most obvious precondition of a relationship — that the person is alive — have changed every definition in their own favor.

Well-known networks do have particular rules for what happens to dead users’ accounts. Still, this is an important social and even philosophical phenomenon. With the countless copies we have made of people, how does the idea of death change? You no longer have to be a scientist, a philosopher, or a fierce writer for a trace of you to remain on earth. Everyone has writing on the internet that, after that, never goes away. It may be hard to believe, but a service like [liveson.org](http://liveson.org/) now exists that, by learning people’s behavior on social networks, updates their accounts automatically after death. It tweets in your place, in a way that nobody notices you have probably died.

The question is whether people today still live in the virtual world after death. I think it is not far off, a time when telling these two worlds apart becomes impossible, and people after birth imagine the virtual world as their real one. A time when even the meaning of real and imaginary cannot be separated. Will a day come when death loses its meaning?
