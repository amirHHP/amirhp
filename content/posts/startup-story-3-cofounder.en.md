“A beginning” is a series about the adventures on my work path. Read [part one](/en/posts/startup-story-1-school-scape/) and [part two](/en/posts/startup-story-2-lean-startup/). This part is about a hidden need for starting: a cofounder.

From a point on I felt there was no use working on my idea anymore. After throwing myself at several people, after begging a few times, we even built an app, but the next step was unknown to me. I froze there like a statue. I did not know what to do, and nobody came looking. Often I went limp myself. I needed someone else to carry the work and take me with them, or to tap me if I was not working. The hole where that person should have been was heavy.

Even after an early failure, ideation would not leave me. The thing I had loved since childhood: sinking into daydreams, thinking of a world I would change. I would sit for hours, pretending to study, and draw a revolutionary phone with a different user experience, imagining I would manufacture it and shake the market.

One day, in the middle of those daydreams, I was on a bus, sick of morning traffic. In the dream I pulled people out of their private cars, and while they twisted in the air, waving their hands to get back to their lonely shells and put their foot down, indifferent to society, I put them in one car and traffic was solved, just like that. I thought: why don’t all these people with repeating routes share the empty seats instead of riding alone? That is when a complicated startup idea arrived.

The bus was going to the 1395 Thursday market. Android developers gathered for two days to remember they were real people, and what better chance to find a programmer for my supposedly brilliant idea. Until your network and reputation are large enough that a partner appears as easily as drinking water, events like that are usually the only way to find a cofounder.

The last experience had shown that without a full-time cofounder I could not take a single step. In that crowd I saw a university friend. I started talking, and I was tempted to tell him the idea that had occupied me for days: single-occupant cars sharing empty seats. On paper it was very attractive, with endless dreaming. It got more interesting because that spring of 1394 Snapp was slowly taking off and Tapsi had not started yet.

He said he would think for a few days. I was very hopeful we would build it together. I decided this time to focus on what I already had: content and relationships.

A week later he sent a no. Everything felt dull, but he introduced someone who had already started. A spring evening was my first meeting with Hamed Lashkari and Mibarim, two months after they had launched. Strange moments. I had never gone this far. My ideas always died in the womb, and now I had someone who was not only as motivated as I was, but more, to push the work and make the startup succeed.

I tried, with what I had learned in the three-day Lean Startup Machine, to evaluate the shared-car idea again. We went among people a few times and asked about carpooling, though we were not harsh enough in the evaluation and we read every piece of feedback as positive in the end (I will write soon about how to evaluate an idea properly).

Our redesigned product, full of illusion and copying from foreign examples, shipped two months later and was unveiled on the first of Shahrivar at the Smart City exhibition. A product everyone liked and nobody could use.
