People keep mixing up my photo with Saber’s. That, I think, commits me to a few notes about these years and about the work he actually did. Why does Saber’s work matter more than the way people talk about it?



What I’m writing comes from the years I was a journalist: a lot of time spent writing, an interview with Behdad, and the reading I did then to finish that interview and a report on Persian type.



Which year? 1392. Journalism had tied me more tightly to writing. Where did we write? Microsoft Word. Or, like me, OpenOffice. When you stare at a blank Word page five hours a day, which font you use becomes the job’s main anxiety.



What fonts were there? As always, Tahoma led the Persian fonts. It was almost everywhere: Blogfa, PersianBlog, Cloob… any Persian site you can think of. Even on FriendFeed, Twitter, and Facebook, people forced Tahoma onto the text with Stylish and a handful of extensions.



What else? Arial? And Times New Roman, a clumsy typeface that at least existed everywhere. For email or a blog, everyone still switched to Tahoma as soon as they could, because it was at least more readable.



And then the B-series fonts, with no clear makers. With the tech of those years they weren’t available on the web either. From books, handouts, papers, magazines, and newspapers, almost everyone used them. They came from the 1380s — the post-Maryam years — when people could slowly choose their own type. None of them were fit for the web, for better monitors, for more color and more pixels.



In the middle of that, Adobe Arabic, BBC Nasim, and Droid Naskh were three newer fonts. Technically more modern. Not as crooked as Arial, not as dizzy and looping as Tahoma, not as wrecked as the B series. But none of them were Persian. Why? They had traits that made us think of Arabic type. The first was officially Arabic. The second was designed by an Arab designer. The third was Android’s system font for Arabic.



Around those same days in 1392 another font, Iranian Sans, was being passed around. It looked like an attempt at a standard typeface, but it had no identity. We used it anyway. Some of it was crooked; you could tell something wasn’t working.



So you see. We were in days when there was none. I mean **none**. There was no standard Persian font in the world. That is where Saber walked in. He took the open-source DejaVu font that lived on Linux then, tuned it, and made a Persian font from it. A font has two sides: the look, and the implementation.



Saber did a few things: 1. For the first time an Iranian, a Persian speaker, was designing a font for the modern web. 2. For the first time someone was looking at the technical and engineering side as well. 3. For the first time the font was released as open source. 4. It may be one of the rare times anywhere that a font stayed this under development: from 1394 until today, 33 versions.



Saber was not a designer. He was more of an engineer. But his commitment, his belief in open source, and the constant work meant that from 1394 until now, Vazir — along with six or seven other fonts — quietly became the typeface we had dreamed of.



He didn’t stop there. The Microsoft Word years were over. Everyone was writing and reading online, and we still had to use Arabic fonts like Lateef, Lalezar, and Markazi, the only usable ones on Google Docs. Saber kept pushing to get his font onto Google so it would reach everyone more easily.



Other things happened in between. In 1395, Iran font shipped as a modern, designed, premium typeface. Around then there was also an attempt to give Wikipedia its own font, done by one of Iran’s major type designers. In the end, after a lot of noise, interviews, and fuss, the result was not what people had hoped. Saber, with no talk, no interview, no photo, kept shipping version after version of Vazir. No claim. No thought of income. No “enough, after all these years I should at least make some money” or even “enough, I’ve been working on a font for free for two years and shipped this many versions.” How does someone work on something for eight years, past themselves, knowing nothing comes back?
