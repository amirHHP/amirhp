“Astonishing” is surely the only word that, at the beginning, the middle, and the end of *The Great Gatsby*, keeps wandering in your mind. A wondrous story with magical descriptions, and you are often confused about how these words reached Scott Fitzgerald. A book you feel is extraordinary at every moment, but when you reach the end you see that the earlier extraordinariness falls short next to this sudden ending. An ending that, very quietly, without your noticing at all, swallows you.
Note: this piece may contain spoilers. It may not.

One of the largest faults you can find with most Iranian stories is that the writer’s spirit does not run through them. They are only tales being told. *The Great Gatsby* is a novel in which the writer is not a mere narrator. The whole novel is mysterious. Even after you finish the book you do not understand who Gatsby was and how he made all that money, because everything said about him has been said by others, and for the narrator he is the “great” Gatsby. When “Tom” (in a way Gatsby’s enemy) says he has found out what Gatsby does, you pass over his words without believing them. However hard you try, you cannot put Gatsby in the boxes of good and bad. I tried for hours and failed. In the end my mind leaned toward the narrator, and I like him without meaning to.

> The picture Scott Fitzgerald draws of Gatsby is a picture made of hints and counter-hints, of rumors and allusions, of unbelievable facts and listen-able lies, of guests whispering in each other’s ears.

Probably after you finish the book the first thought that comes is that you have to read it again. I decided that before reading the last chapter I would watch the 2013 *Great Gatsby* film, and I am glad I did, because it had a surprising effect on how I understood the story. Watching the film after the book helped me in two large ways. First, it helped me understand parts I had not grasped well. Second, the film’s faults did not damage my sense of the story. *The Great Gatsby* is wrapped in a veil of literary description much more than other books I have read, and often that pushes the characters and the plot to the margin. While watching the film you have the chance, without those asides, to attend only to the story and understand the accounts you had read. The descriptions and the magical atmosphere I mentioned are among the most tangible points, clearly visible in the film. The space is extremely crowded and colorful, and the set and costume design are an important feature. Scenes that, because of mystery, are treated very briefly in the book are among the film’s most lasting sequences. For example the shooting at the end, or the description of the ash heap between Long Island and New York that Nick constantly passed.

> Gatsby is a representative of 1920s America, of a nation at the peak of pride and self-confidence, stained with corruption, but on the way to reaching the stars. Gatsby is the manifestation of everything that is in a particular, unique, and splendid way American. He is a symbol of a national flowering born of the country’s hidden talents. In short he is the sample, ideal American hero.

Still, the film had many weaknesses next to the story. While reading I found Jay Gatsby a calm, shy, inward person. From the start the closest person who came to mind was Humphrey Bogart, the lead in *Casablanca*. DiCaprio is a character completely against those traits. In *Inception*, *Shutter Island*, *The Departed*, *The Wolf of Wall Street* he has a charismatic, rebellious character, and he is hard to believe in this role.

> The truth is that Jay Gatsby, resident of West Egg on Long Island, was born of his own Platonic conception of himself. Like Charles Foster Kane, he is an imaginary, story-like emblem of another era of America’s economic boom. Gatsby, though he lived at the heart of a corrupt and corrupting way, keeps his innocence and to the end remains a stubborn, undying romantic being whose driving and holding force is love for Daisy, what Fitzgerald calls “the colossal vitality of his illusion.” Gatsby finally, with not the least regret, pays a heavy price for having “lived too long with a single dream.”

Until you read the last chapter, the great Gatsby is an ordinary man who was once in love and has now, after five years, found his love again. It is in reading the last chapter that the great Gatsby takes shape, that you go over everything from the beginning and understand that he was nothing but a dream. He appeared from nowhere, won his love, but because he had nothing he did not reach her, spent five years doing everything to one day close his fist on that green light across the water, and at the end… as he had come, he went. Even the moment of his death is lost in the story. As if he had come in order not to stay. He came alone and left alone and faded among his dreams. The great Gatsby, more than a human being, was a dream, and maybe only a morning fog.

> Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us. It eluded us then, but that’s no matter — tomorrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther… And one fine morning — So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past.
