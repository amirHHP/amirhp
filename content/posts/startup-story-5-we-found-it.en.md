In [part one](/en/posts/startup-story-1-school-scape/) I wrote about my last years of school, how I tried to run a magazine for real, and how that became my first journalism. In [part two](/en/posts/startup-story-2-lean-startup/) I wrote about a strange afternoon when, with no plan, I fell into starting a company. An idea that came first and never went beyond an idea. In [part three](/en/posts/startup-story-3-cofounder/) I wrote about why a cofounder matters, and how with Hamed the idea left our heads and became something we could run. In [part four](/en/posts/startup-story-4-deep-diving/) I left a steady job to make the shared-car dream real.

A week after leaving Peivast and saying goodbye to journalism (though I tried then to keep my contacts and build an independent outlet, and finally decided it was a waste of time), in Bahman 1395 I started full-time on Mibarim at Dymond. Once I left the job I started looking at the data we had gathered. I called our customers and learned that nobody understood how to use the app. Some had only tested it, and that test was logged, so many drivers were sending requests to people who were only testing and were not active users.

We were completely lost. What we wrongly thought was that if our user count went up, the platform would form by itself, and worse, that we could raise that count with ads. We knew nothing about CAC and LTV, so we did not know “at what price users grow” or, if they did grow, “how long they stay and how much they earn.” We also had no real sense of a funnel. We did not see that acquisition is only the first step. To grow a platform you have to give both sides a reason to stay — why use it when it does not work yet? On a platform like Mibarim, tangled with time and route, that was even harder. The lack of a strong mentor was felt from day one. We did not know what to do, and nobody told us how to get out of the mess.

Until the end of 1395 we tried, in a lot of chaos, to grow users cheaply. We succeeded at that, but it did not turn into money. People did not even complete trips.

The app worked like this: a passenger or a driver entered their fixed route and daily time, and immediately saw similar routes from others. After finding a match they sent a request. By that point a large number of users had already left the funnel. Now imagine that a large share of requests never got an answer. The one or two percent who found a match arranged things in the in-app chat — and then? We had not thought about after. Reaching that stage was so hard we had never felt the need. If someone reached the last step, we did not know whether they took the trip, or what happened to our money.

After phone calls with users, three months of looking and worrying, we concluded that the current app had a few large problems:

* Our driver persona was completely different from what we thought. We assumed we could turn an ordinary person who had never picked up a passenger, or had done it a few times, into a driver. In our eyes driver and passenger were the same, and both used one app. We learned that our driver users were people who already did this at least once a day. So we split the apps, to target different people and make acquisition easier, because each had a different channel.
* The app, for the reason above, had become very complex. The user (driver or passenger) entered every day’s plan: which day, what time to work, what time home. We had done this for convenience, so they would enter it once and not touch the app every day. The problem is that few people go to work on a repeating schedule, and those who are that busy will not involve themselves in something like this.
* Users entering their daily info once, then not updating it, filled the system with invalid data.
* In the calls we went to people who had reached the last step but did not take the trip. We learned they had connected, but they could not agree on who would go to whom.

Looking at the processes, we decided the app had to be much simpler. In Ordibehesht 1396 we shipped a new MVP. We learned not to spend too much time on the product at this stage. Hamed had the new app ready to test in a month. In the new version we introduced a virtual station so users would not fight over who goes to whom (you may not believe it, but in our charming culture the passenger expected the driver at their door, and the driver expected the passenger to come to them). We also cut booking to two steps.

In that time we also learned more about platforms: to start one, you have to create one side artificially.

The new structure: open the app, see trips, book with one click.

With those changes we completed close to two hundred trips in four days at Elecomp 1396. With those trips we wanted to test a few things.

   * Is the new MVP better than the old product?
   * Will anyone request a car from an unknown brand?
   * Will anyone walk to a pickup point?
   * Will anyone request a car online for someone else?

In the end all of those hypotheses were confirmed.

After a lot of looking, our model moved closer to line taxis and away from carpooling. We became a new kind of line taxi without a station.

In a mature ecosystem a startup at this stage should raise something like 700 million to a billion tomans. We thought two hundred trips a day was enough to get investors’ attention. None of the rivals had a system agile enough to do that volume in four days.

Most Iranian investors, though, saw one thing: when do I get the money back?

I am not saying that attention is wrong, but an investor’s main ability should be seeing a future the founders could not see. Return on money is the simplest issue; even a traditional bazaar trader can be convinced that a fivefold return in five years is the best investment.
