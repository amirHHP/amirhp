In [part one](/en/posts/startup-story-1-school-scape/) I wrote about my last years of school, how I tried to run a magazine for real, and how that became my first journalism. In [part two](/en/posts/startup-story-2-lean-startup/) I wrote about a strange afternoon when, with no plan, I fell into starting a company. An idea that came first and never went beyond an idea. In [part three](/en/posts/startup-story-3-cofounder/) I wrote about why a cofounder matters, and how with Hamed the idea left our heads and became something we could run.

The exhibition feedback made us far too hopeful. A kind of illusion of being important. Us? At an official fair? With our own booth? Across from Snapp? There was a lot of feedback, from safety to how do we sign up? Places like that are full of talk and ideas, and resisting those ideas becomes one of your most important skills.

Our attempt to raise 70 million from angel investors went nowhere (70 million on a day when the base salary was 1 million). One day we went to a manager at Bank Keshavarzi who wanted to “take the whole startup” and give “as much money as it needed.” We went to a billboard contractor who wanted to fill the Chalus road with Mibarim and, in two weeks of Nowruz, make all of Tehran know us. In the end it was never clear why he would not pay fifty million and preferred to import hard drives, because he had heard that in a few months the national intranet would launch and people would be desperate for offline internet(!).

I tried to get help from friends. When you start a company, the first people who help are usually colleagues you have a good relationship with. They know your domain and their minds are closer to yours. So my circle at Peivast magazine became the first members of the Mibarim team.

With the illusion we had, we tried hard not to go to an accelerator and to start with an angel. Their coldness made us feel we had no choice. Later we understood how little we knew about starting a business.

Avatech rejected us in autumn 1395. They were right. We had no real slides or pitch, and we did not know the concepts well. Three months later, with their feedback, we went to Dymond. Dymond not only took us, they invested 65 million instead of their usual 25, for 5 percent more equity. Our confidence was at its peak. When we looked at other teams — students with no experience, no knowledge, not even a short work history — we felt how far ahead we were.

In Bahman 1395 I left Peivast. I had many reasons:

+ Journalism had become too uniform. Every month I wrote the same section in the same way. The frames were fully set, and it was hard to go beyond them.
+ I had doubts about journalism in Iran, one of my teenage dreams. Even at Peivast, which was not a political paper, you could not write freely about everything. Money and relationships usually decided. (Which company bought an ad or a reportage? Who are we friends with, who are we enemies with?)
+ The Pidad project, my main reason for staying at Peivast, was fading. It had no budget. The people we had hired for it were moved to other teams, and I was left with myself.
+ The multimedia and new work I wanted (like adding video to a print magazine) was not seen at all after we did it, and the organization had gone flat.
+ And of course I had a solid excuse called Mibarim, which gave me motive and nerve.
