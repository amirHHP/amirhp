Sometimes some ideas are hard to explain to others. For people who have used proprietary software like Windows and Mac for years, and who every time they download a program look for a crack and a way around the author’s rights, it is hard to explain a word like “LUG,” or to talk about open-source gatherings. Even to many people who are specialists in software it is hard to make them see that in Tehran, for ten years, a group of people gathered, and as if they had lost each other for a long time they made plans, and with an indescribable thirst talked about their love for a class of software. Harder still is saying that this group lasted ten years in one breath, people came and went, and the thirst stayed in place. Some who were teenagers became young and, following that thirst, joined. You can look at the reason for that thirst and that lasting from several sides.

First, free software forces you not to be a mere consumer. In your first days in the open-source world, when you look for solutions to your problems, you see that a lot of people have shared their experience with you, and some spend hours every day solving beginners’ problems. Unavoidably, after you have become experienced in that world, you become one of those users who like to take others’ hands, and this endless cycle continues, because the thinking was built from the ground as voluntary.

Second is mutual respect. Free software does not look at you as a customer. So you do not look at it as a seller. Microsoft and Apple and Adobe and the others always looked at you as a customer. You were sometimes happy that with cleverness and effort you downloaded the latest Photoshop, and lately you enjoyed Microsoft sending updates for your non-genuine Windows. In the open-source world you always remain a respected user of this ecosystem. From day one nobody had a right to limit a service on you. You use the services because the services belong to nobody, and everyone stands on the shoulders of the person before. In truth nobody owns free software, and everyone owns free software.

Third, you are not a commodity in this ecosystem. So you do not behave like one. It is not strange, then, if at [Tehlug](http://tehlug.org/)’s tenth year, with no profit in the middle, you face an event where about ten people give their knowledge to others for free and run the most serious, highest-quality workshops. In this creed, the freedom of knowledge is a principle. Its founder’s first cry was that we must not, by closing code, block the progress of software knowledge.

You might choose to call this behavior software socialism. With this difference: it comes from society, not from government, and everything is done voluntarily, not by force. A stable socialist community, with all its benefits and away from all its harms. A condition in which the person serves the community because they use others’ service, and their service, turning with the days and riding the roaring waves of society, comes back toward them.
